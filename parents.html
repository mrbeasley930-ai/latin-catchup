Half-Term Catch-Up: parent pack

This page is for whoever is at home with Noah over half term. It explains what the catch-up is, what it asks of him each day, and what you can do to help without knowing any Latin. You can read it on a phone, or print it from your browser’s menu.

What this is

Since mid-September Noah has been doing his own Focus Lessons in Latin, one-to-one with a workbook, while the rest of the class went on with the main course. That has worked: his most recent one came back at 134 out of 171. It has also meant he has not sat nine of the class’s lessons: four on comparing things in Latin (“braver”, “bravest”, “better”, “best”), two on the words for “this” and “that”, two on Roman dinner parties, and the Chapter 7 vocabulary.

The catch-up is a website that gives him those nine lessons one a day over the two weeks, rebuilt at his level. Each day is about 40 minutes: a page of notes to read and type up, ten short questions marked on the spot with the reason, and two or three sentences to write out and check against a model answer. Nothing is timed and nothing has to be handed in: every answer saves straight to my class record as he goes, so I can see each day what he has done and where he went wrong, and I will pick those things up with him in the first lesson back.

The link: the same web address works on any computer, tablet or phone, at home or elsewhere. He signs in with class code Y10 and his first name, exactly as he does at school. If he stops halfway through a day, it picks up where he left off, on any device.

What it asks of home

Three things, and the first is the one that matters.

  1. A fixed slot. The same 40 minutes each weekday, at a time when he is not tired and the computer is free. Mornings tend to go better than evenings for this kind of work. One day a day; if a day is missed, he does the next day’s page the next day and does not try to do two.
  2. Someone nearby for the first ten minutes. That is the notes-typing part, which is the bit most likely to be skipped. You do not need to read the Latin. You need to see that the notes file is open and that something is going into it.
  3. Two questions at the end. “Can you show me the tick?” (the day’s tab at the top of the page gets a green tick when the day is finished) and “What was today’s rule?” (each day ends with one rule in a box; he reads it to you). The day-by-day list further down gives you the rule in plain English, so you know what the answer should sound like.

Things it does not ask: sitting with him through the whole 40 minutes, checking his answers, or doing the vocabulary with him unless you both want to. The site marks everything itself and shows him the model answers.

If he is stuck

  • Under every question there is a grey line that gives the meaning of every Latin word in it. The answer is never a word he has not been given; it is only ever a choice of ending.
  • The notes page for the day has the answer to every question on that day. “Look at the table you just typed” is the right thing to say.
  • The written sentences have a Show the model answer button. He writes first, then looks, then gives himself 0, 1 or 2. Honest marking matters more than a high mark: I read what he wrote, not just the number.
  • If the page shows “saved on this device” instead of “saved to class store”, the internet connection has dropped. Nothing is lost; it sends when the connection comes back.
  • If something is actually broken, email me at school and carry on with the next day.

The notes file

Instead of copying notes into an exercise book, Noah types them into one Word document on his school OneDrive, called Latin Catch-Up Notes. He sets it up before Day 1 (the website has a page that walks him through it) and adds a section each day: a heading, a table, a rule in bold and one example. This is the part of the fortnight that does most of the work, which is why the questions on each day do not unlock until he has ticked that the notes are typed. If you look at one thing, look at this file: ten headings by the end of the fortnight, one per day.

Day by day: the rule, and what to ask

For each day: the rule in plain English, so you know what he should be able to tell you; one question to ask him with its answer; and what a full-marks written answer looks like, in case you want to see it. The Latin is there for completeness; you do not need to say it.

Day 1 · Comparatives

The class did this as Lesson 6.

The rule: to say “braver” or “more stupid”, Latin adds -ior to the adjective. “Than” is quam, and the word after quam has the same ending as the thing being compared.

Ask: What does -ior on the end of a word mean?  Answer: “more” or “-er”, as in braver, happier.
A full-marks sentence: dominus stultior est quam servus = The master is more stupid than the slave. (The commonest slip is to put it the wrong way round.)

Day 2 · Than, two ways

Lesson 7.

The rule: Latin can also say “than” with no quam at all, by changing the ending of the other person (the “ablative” ending: -a, -o or -e). multo in front means “much”. Never both quam and the ablative together.

Ask: How many ways are there to say “than”?  Answer: Two: quam, or no quam and the ablative ending.
A full-marks sentence: puella laetior est servo = The girl is happier than the slave.

Day 3 · Superlatives

Lesson 8.

The rule: “very brave” or “the bravest” is -issimus. Two small groups change it (pulcherrimus, facillimus). A word meaning “of all” after it makes it “the most … of all”. -ior = more; -issimus = most.

Ask: fortior or fortissimus: which one is “bravest”?  Answer: fortissimus. (fortior is braver.)
A full-marks sentence: puella pulcherrima omnium est = The girl is the most beautiful of all.

Day 4 · The irregulars

Lesson 10.

The rule: five common words do not follow the pattern, just as English says good, better, best. They have to be learnt: bonus, melior, optimus (good) · malus, peior, pessimus (bad) · magnus, maior, maximus (big) · parvus, minor, minimus (small) · multus, plus, plurimus (much). English words help: optimist, pessimist, major, minor, maximum, minimum.

Ask: Good, better, best in Latin?  Answer: bonus, melior, optimus. (Any one of the five sets is a fair question.)
A full-marks sentence: rex maximus omnium est = The king is the greatest of all.

Day 5 · Dinner Parties 1

Lesson 4. In English.

The facts: the Roman day ran from sunrise to sunset in twelve hours, so an hour was longer in summer than winter; the sixth hour was always midday. Three meals: a tiny breakfast, a cold snack at midday, and the cena from about the ninth hour, the one meal that mattered. The two written answers are about a poem by Martial that goes through the day hour by hour; a good answer makes a point and backs it with a detail from the poem.

Ask: What was the main meal called, and roughly when did it start?  Answer: The cena, from about the ninth hour (mid-afternoon).
A full-marks written answer gives two things Romans did before dinner, with the hour of each, and quotes or closely paraphrases the line of the poem each comes from.

Day 6 · Superlative adverbs

Lesson 11.

The rule: an adjective describes a person or thing (very brave soldiers); an adverb describes how something is done (they fought very bravely). The adverb ends in -e instead of -us and never changes. quam celerrime = as quickly as possible.

Ask: What does quam celerrime mean?  Answer: As quickly as possible.
A full-marks sentence: servi optime laborant = The slaves work very well.

Day 7 · hic, haec, hoc

Lesson 12.

The rule: hic, haec, hoc means “this” and changes its ending to match the word it goes with: this man, this girl, these boys. Two odd forms: huius = of this, huic = to this. Latin his means “to these”, never English “his”.

Ask: What does hic, haec, hoc mean?  Answer: This (and these).
A full-marks sentence: hic puer servos vocat = This boy calls the slaves.

Day 8 · ille, illa, illud

Lesson 13, the last before half term.

The rule: ille, illa, illud means “that” and works exactly like hic. Its endings are the ordinary adjective endings with ill- in front, apart from illius = of that and illi = to that. hic = this (near me); ille = that (over there).

Ask: Which one is “this” and which is “that”?  Answer: hic is this; ille is that.
A full-marks sentence: ille servus cibum portat = That slave carries the food.

Day 9 · Dinner Parties 2

Lesson 9. In English.

The facts: the Roman dining room (triclinium, “three couches”) had three couches round a table in a U, three diners on each, nine places numbered 1 to 9. The middle couch was the couch of honour; place 6 on it was the best seat, and the host lay next to it at place 7. Diners lay on the left elbow with the head towards the table. Where you lay showed your rank. He draws the plan and puts it in his notes.

Ask: How many couches, and which number was the best place?  Answer: Three couches; place 6, next to the host at 7.
A full-marks written answer names two guests from the Horace passage and where each lay, quoting the line; and, for the second question, says what Pliny did differently from the mean host (served everyone the same) and why, with a quotation.

Day 10 · Vocabulary and review

The Chapter 7 words the class was tested on in two halves.

What it is: the 51 words typed into the notes file as a two-column list, then a 24-word check on the site (he types the English meaning; it marks itself) and six questions that mix up the whole fortnight. To learn the list afterwards: cover the English column, say the meaning, uncover, tick or cross; do the crosses again the next day. If you want to help with something, this is the easy one: read a Latin word from his list and he gives the meaning.

Ask: any word from his list. Five to try: optimus = best; crudelis = cruel; flumen = river; sine = without; nihil = nothing.

After half term

In the first Latin lesson back I will have his ten days of answers in front of me and will go through what did not land. There is no test on return. The notes file he has built is his revision file for this grammar from now on, so it should come back to school on his laptop or be shared with me on Teams.
