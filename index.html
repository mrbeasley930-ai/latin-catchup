What this is

Over the last half term your class covered nine lessons that you did not sit, because you were doing your Focus Lessons instead. This catch-up takes those nine lessons and gives you one a day, in a shape that suits you: short, with every Latin word explained on the page, and nothing timed.

Each day takes about 40 minutes. Do one day a day, in order, with a break in the middle if you want one. If you miss a day, do not try to do two the next day: just carry on with the next one. Days 5 and 9 are civilisation days, in English, and are a change of pace.

How each day works

1. Read the notes and type them into your notes file. Every day starts with the page the class copied into their exercise books. You type it instead, into one notes file on your computer. This page shows you how to set the file up — read it before Day 1. The questions unlock when you tick that the notes are typed.

2. Answer the questions. Ten (or eight) questions, one attempt each, marked straight away with the reason. The grey line under every question gives you every Latin word you need, so you are never guessing a word: you are only ever choosing an ending.

3. Write it up. Two or three short sentences to translate or build, with a model answer to check against and marks you give yourself honestly. Mr Beasley reads these afterwards.

Day 1 · Comparatives: braver, happier, more stupid

Lesson 6 · about 40 minutes · 10 questions, 3 written

What the class did. Lesson 6 was the first new grammar of the year: the comparative adjective — the -er / more form. English says braver, happier, more stupid. Latin adds -ior to the adjective. Then it says than with the word quam.

You already know how to find the doer (nominative) and the object (accusative). Today only adds one new ending, -ior, and one new word, quam. Every verb below is est (is) or sunt (are).

Day 2 · Than without quam: the ablative of comparison, and multo

Lesson 7 · about 40 minutes · 10 questions, 3 written

What the class did. Lesson 7 took quam away. Latin has a second way to say than: leave quam out and put the other person into the ablative case. servus stultior est domino = the slave is more stupid than the master. The class also met multo = much, as in ‘much braver’.

The ablative is a case you have seen but not used much. Today you only need its singular endings: -a (1st declension), -o (2nd) and -e (3rd), and the plurals -is, -is, -ibus.

1 · Read the notes, then type them into your notes file

Read the page below twice. Then open your notes file and type the things in the list underneath it. Typing them is the point: it is how they go in. How to set up the notes file.

[today’s date]Two Ways to Say THAN — and multo
1. With quam (Day 1): same ending both sides. servus stultior est quam dominus.
2. Without quam — the ablative of comparison: drop quam, put the other person into the ablative. servus stultior est domino. = The slave is more stupid than the master.
ablative1st (puella)2nd (servus)3rd (miles, rex)
onepuellaservomilite, rege
manypuellisservismilitibus, regibus
3. multo = much, by much: multo stultior = much more stupid. It sits in front of the comparative and never changes.
Three traps. (a) Never quam AND the ablative together. (b) A 1st-declension ablative (puella) looks like a nominative: find the doer by the verb first. (c) An ablative with a preposition in front (in villa, cum servo) is never ‘than’.

Day 3 · Superlatives: very brave, the bravest

Lesson 8 · about 40 minutes · 10 questions, 3 written

What the class did. Lesson 8 added the superlative: -est / most / very. Latin adds -issimus to the genitive stem: fortissimus = very brave, the bravest. It then takes the endings of bonus, bona, bonum, so it agrees with its noun like any ordinary adjective.

Two small groups change the ending: adjectives ending in -er (pulcher → pulcherrimus) and facilis / difficilis (→ facillimus). And a ‘genitive plural’ after it (omnium, of all) turns ‘very’ into ‘the most … of’.

[today’s date]The Roman Day — and its Three Meals
A Roman day ran from sunrise to sunset and was cut into twelve hours. An hour was a twelfth of that day’s daylight, so it was long in summer (about 75 minutes) and short in winter (about 44 minutes). hora sexta (the sixth hour) was always midday.

Day 7 · hic, haec, hoc: this, these

Lesson 12 · about 40 minutes · 10 questions, 3 written

What the class did. Lesson 12 learnt the word for this: hic, haec, hoc. It changes its ending to agree with its noun, like an adjective: hic vir = this man, hanc puellam = this girl (object), hi pueri = these boys.

The table looks long. You only need to be sure of the doer and object rows today (nominative and accusative), singular and plural, and two odd forms: huius = of this, huic = to this. The plural is bonus with h- in front: hi, hae, haec.

The facts. triclinium = “three couches” (Greek treis + kline). Three couches (lecti) round one low table (mensa) in a U; the fourth side is open so slaves can serve. Each couch holds three, so nine places, numbered 1–9 round the U. On every couch the places are summus (top), medius (middle), imus (bottom).
Rank. The couch of honour is the lectus medius. Its bottom place, 6, is the locus consularis, the place of honour. The host lies next to it at 7, the top of the lectus imus, so he can talk to his chief guest.
Reclining. On the left elbow, head towards the table, right hand free to eat. Slaves stood and served; children sat. Sandals off, a slave washed your feet, you brought your own napkin (mappa).
Two words. umbra = a “shadow”: an uninvited guest brought along by an important man. libertus = a freedman, a freed slave.
